<div class="kicker">THE THEORY / 02</div>

<h2>Just because the message gets there doesn't mean it connects.</h2>

<p class="lead">Have you ever gotten a text that technically said all the right things, but something about it just felt off? Communication theory can help explain why.</p>

<h2>First, how does communication actually work?</h2>

<p>Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver developed a model that looks at communication as the transmission of information. Basically, someone has something to say, sends a message through a channel, and someone else receives it.</p>

<p>There can also be noise, which is anything that gets in the way of the message getting through correctly.</p>

<p>It's a pretty straightforward way to understand communication, and it actually explains why AI can be so helpful.</p>

<div class="callout">
<strong>Think about it:</strong> You know exactly what you want to say in an email, but you can't figure out how to word it. You ask ChatGPT to help, and suddenly you have a clear, professional email ready to send. In terms of getting information across, AI did its job.
</div>

<h2>But there's more to communication than that.</h2>

<p>Warren Weaver described three different levels of communication problems:</p>

<div class="lens-grid">
  <div class="lens-card">
    <strong>01 / Technical</strong>
    <span>Did the message actually get delivered correctly?</span>
  </div>

  <div class="lens-card">
    <strong>02 / Semantic</strong>
    <span>Did the person understand what you meant?</span>
  </div>

  <div class="lens-card">
    <strong>03 / Effectiveness</strong>
    <span>Did the message have the effect you wanted?</span>
  </div>
</div>

<p>That third level is where things get especially interesting when AI is involved.</p>

<h2>Let's talk about an AI-written apology.</h2>

<p>Imagine you hurt your friend's feelings. You know you need to apologize, but you aren't sure what to say. So you ask AI to write something thoughtful, copy it, and send it.</p>

<p>Your friend gets the message. It's clear, polite, and sounds sincere. Technically, everything worked.</p>

<p>But what happens if your friend finds out you didn't actually write it?</p>

<p>Maybe they don't care. Or maybe they start wondering whether you meant any of it. Maybe the message suddenly feels less personal, even though the words haven't changed.</p>

<div class="callout">
<strong>Here's the point:</strong> A message can be delivered perfectly and still fail to create the connection someone was hoping for.
</div>

<h2>Where Shannon and Weaver's model falls short</h2>

<p>The transmission model is useful, but it doesn't fully explain the human side of communication. People aren't just machines receiving information. We bring our own memories, emotions, relationships, and experiences into every conversation.</p>

<p>Sometimes a slightly awkward apology that someone actually struggled to write can mean more than a perfectly polished one.</p>

<p>That doesn't mean using AI is automatically wrong. It just means that making communication more efficient isn't always the same thing as making it better.</p>

<h2>So what should we actually do?</h2>

<p>Use AI when you need help making something clearer. But when you're communicating something personal, make sure the feelings and ideas are still yours.</p>

<p>AI can help you figure out how to say something. It shouldn't always be the thing deciding what you have to say.</p>

<div class="callout">
<strong>TRY THIS:</strong> The next time you use AI to help write a personal message, read it back and ask yourself: "Would I actually say this? Does this sound like me?" If the answer is no, change it.
</div>

<h2>The takeaway</h2>

<p>Shannon and Weaver help us understand how messages travel. But the bigger question is what happens once those messages reach another person.</p>

<p>Because communication isn't just about getting the words right. It's about what those words mean to the people involved.</p>
