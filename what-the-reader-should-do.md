<section>
  <div class="kicker">THE COMMUNICATION TOOLKIT / 06</div>

  <h1>Use the tool. Keep your voice.</h1>

  <p class="intro">We've spent a lot of time talking about how AI is changing communication. But what are we actually supposed to do with that information?</p>

  <p>I don't think the answer is to stop using AI. It can be  helpful when you're struggling to organize your thoughts, find the right words, or figure out where to start.</p>
 
  <p>But I also think there's a difference between using AI to help communicate something you already mean and letting it decide what you want to say.</p>

  <p>So instead of coming up with a bunch of random rules, I wanted to take the communication theories we've already covered and think about how we can actually use them in everyday life.</p>
</section>


<section>
  <div class="kicker section-label">RESEARCH INTO PRACTICE</div>

  <h2>Four things communication theory can teach us</h2>

  <p>These theories weren't originally created as instructions for using ChatGPT. The suggestions below are my applications of the research to AI-assisted communication.</p>

  <div class="feature-grid">

    <div class="feature-card">
      <span class="number">01 / SHANNON & WEAVER</span>

      <h3>Clear doesn't always mean connected.</h3>

      <p><strong>The research:</strong> Shannon and Weaver's model explains communication in terms of information being transmitted from a source to a receiver. It also considers problems that can interfere with communication.</p>

      <p><strong>How it relates to AI:</strong> AI can make a message easier to understand, but that doesn't guarantee the other person will feel understood or cared about.</p>

      <p><strong>My takeaway:</strong> Use AI to help with clarity, but don't assume that a perfectly written message automatically creates a meaningful connection.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="feature-card">
      <span class="number">02 / DANIEL CHANDLER</span>

      <h3>Context matters.</h3>

      <p><strong>The research:</strong> Semiotics examines how signs create meaning. Chandler explains how people use social and cultural codes to interpret words, images, symbols, and other signs.</p>

      <p><strong>How it relates to AI:</strong> AI can produce language that sounds personal, but it doesn't share your actual memories or experiences with the person you're communicating with.</p>

      <p><strong>My takeaway:</strong> Think about the context of your relationship. Sometimes an inside joke or a specific memory means more than a polished paragraph.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="feature-card">
      <span class="number">03 / STUART HALL</span>

      <h3>The person reading it gets a say.</h3>

      <p><strong>The research:</strong> Hall's encoding/decoding model explains that people don't always interpret messages the way their creators intended.</p>

      <p><strong>How it relates to AI:</strong> Someone might appreciate an AI-assisted message, while someone else might feel uncomfortable knowing AI helped write it.</p>

      <p><strong>My takeaway:</strong> Think about who you're communicating with, not just what you want to say. Their experiences and expectations matter, too.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="feature-card">
      <span class="number">04 / EVIA & O'KEEFE</span>

      <h3>Who's actually shaping the message?</h3>

      <p><strong>The research:</strong> Evia and O'Keefe's concept of <em>intentio algorithmi</em> draws attention to the influence algorithms have on the creation of AI-assisted content.</p>

      <p><strong>How it relates to AI:</strong> When ChatGPT suggests ideas, wording, or tone, it can influence what you end up communicating, even when you're the one making the final decision.</p>

      <p><strong>My takeaway:</strong> Pay attention to whether AI is helping you express your own thoughts or coming up with the thoughts for you.</p>
    </div>

  </div>
</section>

<section>
  <div class="kicker section-label">BEFORE YOU HIT SEND</div>

  <h2>Four questions worth asking</h2>

  <p>After looking at these theories, I think there are a few questions we should consider before sending something AI helped us write.</p>

  <p>These aren't part of a formal communication model. They're questions I've developed from the theories discussed throughout this primer.</p>

  <ol class="checklist">
    <li><strong>Is the message clear?</strong> Shannon and Weaver remind us that getting information across matters. Does the message actually communicate what you intended?</li>

    <li><strong>Does the context matter?</strong> Semiotics reminds us that words can have different meanings depending on relationships, experiences, and culture. Is there something personal AI might miss?</li>

    <li><strong>How might the other person interpret it?</strong> Hall reminds us that we can't completely control how someone else understands our message. Could they read it differently than we expect?</li>

    <li><strong>How much did AI influence it?</strong> Evia and O'Keefe remind us that algorithms can shape what gets created. Did AI help you express your ideas, or did it come up with the ideas itself?</li>
  </ol>

  <p>I don't think there's one correct answer to these questions. But I do think they're worth considering, especially when the message is personal or important.</p>
</section>


<section>
  <div class="kicker section-label">THE FINAL TAKEAWAY</div>

  <h2>Keep the human element.</h2>

  <p>One thing I've realized while working on this primer is that communication is a lot more complicated than just finding the right words.</p>

  <p>AI can help us write more clearly, brainstorm new ideas, and communicate things we might otherwise struggle to explain. And I don't think we should ignore those benefits.</p>

  <p>But I also think there's something valuable about coming up with our own ideas, making mistakes, and figuring out how to express ourselves.</p>

  <p>Sometimes a message doesn't need to be perfectly written to mean something. Sometimes the effort, the memories, and the person behind it are what matter most.</p>

  <p>So I don't think the question is whether we should use AI or not. I think the better question is how much of our own creativity and communication we're comfortable giving to it.</p>

  <div class="callout">
    <strong>The takeaway:</strong>
    AI can help us communicate, but we still have to decide what we want to say, what we mean, and how much of ourselves we're putting into the message.
  </div>

  <p><small><strong>Research behind this section:</strong> Shannon and Weaver, <em>The Mathematical Theory of Communication</em>; Daniel Chandler, <em>Semiotics: The Basics</em>; Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model; and Carlos Evia and Sarah O'Keefe, <em>The Uninvited Author</em>.</small></p>
</section>
