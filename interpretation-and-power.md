This page applies Stuart Hall’s encoding/decoding framework to the politics of meaning. A sender encodes a preferred meaning, but an audience decodes it from a social position and may accept, negotiate, or resist it.

## Core idea

Meaning is not guaranteed by the producer. Audiences can read messages in dominant, negotiated, or oppositional ways, depending on social position and context.

## Power dimension

- Platforms, institutions, and media systems shape what becomes "common sense."
- Technical systems and moderation rules can privilege some interpretations over others.
- AI-generated content can amplify dominant framings while obscuring alternatives.

## In the workplace example

Jordan encodes the message as a performance correction. Sam may decode it as a dominant reading (“I am failing and must obey”), a negotiated reading (“the deadlines are real, but the blame is unfair”), or an oppositional reading (“this is surveillance disguised as professionalism”). None of these readings is solved by insisting on Jordan’s private intention.

Power matters because Jordan controls the evaluation and Sam bears the consequences. A responsible message makes room for Sam’s account instead of using polished language to close the discussion.
